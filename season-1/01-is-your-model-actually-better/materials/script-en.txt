SCENE 01: Is the neural model actually better?

Here is a simple tensile test: a machine pulls a metal bar, and we measure how much it stretches. We want to know which of two models predicts that elongation better. A simple regression model and a neural model receive the same loading information and predict the same elongation. The neural model has a smaller average error. Should we replace the simpler model? I'm Ricardo Fitas. We will answer that question using a small paired comparison in Python. The application is a concrete motivation; the five scores in this lesson are synthetic teaching data, not measured results from either model. We will open Python scripts in JupyterLab, execute the calculations, and watch the plots develop. The workflow is to define the target, protect the test set, compare the same cases, quantify the effect, and describe the uncertainty that remains.

SCENE 02: Define the claim before running the code

Start with the intended use. Are you predicting an unseen frame from a familiar specimen, an entirely new specimen, or an unseen operating regime? Those are different claims and usually require different splits. Decide which error matters and what improvement would be useful before examining the results. A small mean reduction may have little value if the worst cases become unacceptable. Conversely, a modest change may matter if it avoids a costly failure in the operating region you care about. State the baseline, the candidate, the unit of evaluation and the information available to both. Record the model revision and the dataset revision. This contract makes the conclusion interpretable and reduces the temptation to change the question after seeing a favourable number.

SCENE 03: Find the independent unit

Suppose each specimen produces one hundred frames. You now have five hundred rows, but only five specimens. Frames from the same tensile-test sequence share geometry, preparation, loading and imaging conditions. Treating every frame as an independent specimen overstates how much new evidence you have. Independence is an assumption about how data arise; it is not a property created by converting them into separate rows. For this worked example, we treat the five hypothetical specimens as independent units. Within each specimen, the frame residuals share a prescribed pattern. We will aggregate frame errors into one score per specimen, then compare the models at that level. This addresses a specimen-level question. Other designs may require subjects, sites, sessions, batches or contiguous time blocks as the evaluation unit.

SCENE 04: Protect selection from evaluation

The same grouping should inform the split. If the deployment target is a new specimen, putting frames from that specimen in both training and testing can leak specimen-specific information. Keep related observations together. Fit preprocessing using training data, select hyperparameters using validation data, and reserve the final evaluation for the claim you specified. If you use cross-validation for model selection, the outer evaluation must remain separate from that selection process. Also be cautious about uncertainty from folds: folds can share most of their training data, so their scores are not automatically independent experiments. Our example begins with fixed hypothetical test-case scores. We are not training a neural network here, and we will not imply that five scores demonstrate a completed nested evaluation. The lesson is to preserve the information boundary before computing uncertainty.

SCENE 06: Understand the estimand

We are calculating the mean of five case-level RMSEs. That is not generally the same as pooling every residual and calculating one global RMSE. With unequal sequence lengths, a pooled metric can give longer sequences more weight. Different weighting schemes answer different questions. Neither is automatically correct for every deployment. Our equal-case average asks how the model performs for a typical case within this fixed teaching set. The mean paired difference of minus zero point zero one five is the effect estimate on that scale. The five lines on the plot show the per-case changes, which the average alone would hide. In this constructed example every line improves. With real data, inspect both helpful and harmful cases and relate their sizes to an engineering or practical threshold.

SCENE 08: A narrow interval can still overpromise

Watch the same bootstrap calculation after one hundred, one thousand, five thousand and twenty thousand resamples. The histogram and interval estimates become numerically more stable, but the number of independent cases remains five. More resampling cannot discover unseen specimen types, operating regimes or shared systematic errors. The interval below zero therefore does not automatically validate the candidate. For this tiny sample, its numerical endpoints do not certify reliable population coverage. Now open the separate central-limit animation in the file browser. Here we sample from a known uniform population and increase the number of independent observations in each average. The standardised distribution of sample means approaches a Gaussian shape. That is a different experiment. Increasing independent sample size is not the same as increasing the number of bootstrap repeats on the same five cases. Our bootstrap distribution has discrete support; we do not impose a Gaussian curve on it. A useful report distinguishes computational approximation, limited independent evidence, and a change in the population or operating conditions.

SCENE 09: An exact paired sign-flip calculation

Open the exact sign-flip script. Under a null model that permits swapping the two model labels within each independent case, how unusual is the observed mean difference? Each of five pairs has two possible label assignments, giving two to the power five, or thirty-two, assignments. Swapping labels changes the sign of one case's difference while preserving the pair. Run the script in the terminal and open its animation. The rows show every assignment of minus one and plus one. Each column still belongs to one case. The next line computes a mean for every row. SCENE 10: The interval and the test ask different questions

The percentile interval is below zero, while the exact two-sided test does not cross a conventional five-percent threshold. This is not a contradiction in the code. The methods use different constructions, and small samples make their limitations visible. In this particular five-pair sign-flip design, even improvements in every case give a smallest attainable two-sided value of zero point zero six two five when the nonzero differences have the same sign. We verified that calculation by enumeration. Do not generalise that number to every statistical test or every dataset with five observations. Also, a p-value is not the probability that the candidate is better. It measures compatibility with a specified null calculation, conditional on its assumptions. Effect size, uncertainty and the practical decision still need to be discussed.

SCENE 11: More frames cannot manufacture new specimens

Return to the residual arrays. Each has shape five by one hundred. We repeat the frames ten times and obtain more columns. The per-case RMSE stays unchanged, and the number of cases stays five. This executed check makes the distinction concrete. Repeated or highly correlated measurements can improve how a particular case is described, but they do not automatically provide more independent cases for generalisation. A frame-level analysis can be appropriate for a frame-level target if its dependence is handled properly. The mistake is to use the number of rows as the sample size for a different population claim. Before choosing a confidence procedure, draw the data hierarchy and decide which unit the conclusion is about.

SCENE 12: Baselines, selection and adverse cases

A comparison also depends on which baselines are included. A neural model should compete with simpler approaches that receive comparable information and are tuned fairly. If you try many candidates and report only the best final test score, the test set has become part of selection. Keep a record of the search and use a protected evaluation or an appropriate nested design. Retain adverse cases, failed fits and predefined exclusion rules. If random initialisation matters, report it separately from variation across specimens: many seeds on the same specimen are not many new specimens. For scientific models, check whether lower observation error comes from recovering the intended mechanism or compensating for missing physics. Better fit and more credible interpretation are related but distinct objectives.

SCENE 13: Turn the evidence into a useful decision

For a practical decision, combine the effect with the cost of obtaining it. You may care about accuracy in a critical regime, robustness, inference latency, memory, training cost or energy. Those claims need their own measurements and a fair protocol; none is supplied by our synthetic error table. A decision threshold should come from the use case rather than from a convenient p-value. You can prefer a model on engineering grounds while stating that evidence for wider generalisation remains limited. You can also decide to collect more independent cases before changing a safety-critical workflow. The report should say what was compared, what information was available, what changed on each case, and what would make you revise the conclusion.

SCENE 15: Should we replace the simpler model?

Return to our opening question: should the neural model replace the simpler model for predicting the elongation of a metal bar in a tensile test? In this teaching example, the candidate reduces mean case-level error from zero point one four eight to zero point one three three, an improvement of about ten percent, and it improves all five cases. That is a promising effect within these synthetic inputs. The illustrative paired bootstrap interval is negative, while the exact two-sided sign-flip calculation gives zero point zero six two five under its null assumptions. Neither calculation proves better performance on untested metal specimens. A defensible next step is to compare the actual models on more independent, protected specimens and measure the relevant accuracy, latency and cost requirements. Prefer the candidate when its practical benefit is supported by that evaluation. Keep the baseline when the improvement is too small or the evidence is insufficient for the intended use.
